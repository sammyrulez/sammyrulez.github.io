# Podcast — Hardware Ate Software. But the Survivors Become Agents

[COLD OPEN]
There's an argument going around that hardware is eating software. That the money in tech is sliding down the stack, into the chips and the data centers. And honestly? Most of the numbers behind it are right. I just think the conclusion is exactly backwards. Because when the bottom of the stack gets cheap, value doesn't sink. It climbs. Let me show you where it goes.

[INTRO]
Hey, I'm Sam. Today I want to argue with a really good essay. It's called "Software Ate the World. Now Hardware Is Eating Software," and it's good enough that it's worth taking seriously — and worth pushing back on. The short version of its thesis is that the center of economic gravity is moving down toward the layers with the hardest physical constraints: the semiconductors, the data platforms, the inference engines that run open models. And the pitch to investors is: stop betting on apps, start betting on silicon. I'm going to agree with almost every fact in that piece, and then completely disagree about what it means. The link to the original is in the show notes if you want to read along.

[CORPO]
Let me set up the argument fairly, because it's a strong one. The claim is that gravity is sliding down the stack. NVIDIA is running gross margins around seventy-five percent. Hyperscaler spending on infrastructure is heading toward something like five point three trillion dollars by the end of the decade. And meanwhile, application margins are getting squeezed — from the seventy-five to ninety percent that software enjoyed in the SaaS era — software as a service — down toward fifty or sixty. So the story writes itself: the value is moving to the bottom.

And part of why it lands is that the hardware underneath us genuinely changed character. For twenty years the substrate was commodity — memory, processors, disk. Cheap, interchangeable, evolving slowly enough that you provisioned it and forgot about it. Nobody thought about it. The AI stack runs on GPUs, on graphics processors, and those are a completely different animal. They move fast, they're expensive, and they carry a fat margin the old commodity parts never did. So when your foundation stops being a boring, swappable input and becomes this scarce, high-margin, fast-aging asset — it feels natural to say, that's where the money lives now. That shift is real. It's the strongest part of the article.

So here's my position in one line: I agree with almost every number. I just disagree about where the arrow points.

Let me be honest about what the article gets right, because I don't want to knock down a strawman. The diagnosis is correct in two big ways.

First, value really is migrating. That comfortable, twenty-year assumption — that the application layer is where the money piles up — that's genuinely breaking. If your product is a thin wrapper around a model call, your margin is a rounding error on someone else's infrastructure bill.

Second — and this is the part that makes people wince — point software is condemned. The single-purpose tool, the app that does one thing behind a form and a database, is getting crushed, exactly the way the article says. That squeeze from ninety percent margins down to fifty? That's real, and it's not a temporary dip. So far, I'm completely on board.

Where I get off the train is the prognosis. The article measures where the capital is going, and then assumes that's where the value gets captured. And those are just not the same thing.

That's the heart of it, so let me slow down on it. The whole argument quietly slides from "this layer is expensive and physically constrained" to "this layer captures the value." But capital intensity and value capture are two different axes. Think about railroads. Brutally capital-intensive to build — and yet most of the lasting value ended up with the businesses that ran on top of the rails, not the ones that laid the track. Being hard to build is not the same as being where the margin lives.

And here's the move the article misses. Commoditization at the bottom doesn't destroy value — it relocates it. When a layer gets cheap and abundant, the barrier drops, and value floods into whatever sits on top and can suddenly do something new.

I keep running into this in practice, not just in theory. A few months back I ran a capable open model — Gemma 4, the thirty-one-billion-parameter one — locally, on a thirty-two-gig Apple Silicon Mac, using Ollama. No tokens, no usage bill, no cloud provider in the loop. Now, the article would file that under "inference is commoditizing, margins are compressing." Fine. But look at what cheap local inference actually did: it put a genuinely capable model on my desk and dropped the cost of building something on top of it to basically zero. That's not value being destroyed. That's the barrier falling — and when a barrier falls, value doesn't evaporate. It moves to whoever's standing on the other side of it. That episode's in the show notes too.

Same with the gap between open and closed models. It closed from about eight percent to under two percent in a single year. The article reads that as a threat — models are commoditizing, so value sinks to whoever owns the compute. I read the exact same fact and hear something else: the intelligence layer just became a utility. And once something's a utility, the interesting question isn't who owns it — it's what you build with it.

Which brings me to the layer the whole thesis forgot. The article lays out five layers: semiconductors, open models and inference, premium models, data platforms, and applications. Notice what's missing. There's no layer for the thing that turns a commodity model into something that actually does work in the world. No orchestration. No integration. No agent. And that omission — that missing layer — is basically the entire disagreement in one gap.

Because the honest thing to say about point software isn't that it's dying. It's that it's metamorphosing. The app stops being a static interface you operate, and becomes an agent that operates on your behalf. Features stop being screens, and become capabilities the agent can reach for. The value was never in the form and the database — it was in the judgment about what to do next. And that judgment is exactly what an agent embodies.

This isn't an abstraction for me. It's most of what I write about. When I keep picking at the tradeoffs between MCP — the Model Context Protocol — and plain skills, or at the way OAuth quietly cracks when you hand it to a model that has no eyes and no hesitation — that's not plumbing beneath the value. That's the scaffolding of the agent layer. How a model reaches the outside world, what it's allowed to touch, how it holds onto a session — in an agent-shaped world, those things aren't details below the value. They are the value. Links to both of those in the show notes.

So if the bottom of the stack is commoditizing — and it is — where does the gravity actually go? Not to the GPUs. You can rent those by the hour, and the whole point of open models and local inference is to turn raw intelligence into a fungible input. Owning silicon is owning the railroad.

The new control points are further up the stack. There are three I'd point to. The first is orchestration — the logic that decides which capability to call, in what order, and how to recover when a step fails. That's genuinely hard, genuinely defensible, and it lives nowhere on that five-layer map. The second is proprietary data loops. The article half-gets this under "data gravity," but it frames data as a moat for platforms, when really it's the fuel that makes an agent good at your specific problem. And the third is agent reliability and safety — the boring, unglamorous work of making an autonomous thing trustworthy enough that you'd actually hand it real permissions. Whoever cracks that owns a kind of lock-in that no amount of capital spending can buy.

And notice: none of those are cheap in capital. They're expensive in judgment, in data, and in the accumulated scar tissue of making agents behave. That's a completely different kind of moat than a chip fab — and it accrues to a completely different kind of builder.

Which is really the key reframe. The article is written for an investor. It's a map of where to put capital across a stack. Read it as an engineer, and the question flips — it's not "where do I put my money," it's "where do I put my effort." And once you flip that lens, almost every conclusion inverts with it. If you believe, like I do, that every app worth keeping is going to become an agent, then the commoditization of hardware and inference isn't the headline. It's the precondition. Cheap, abundant, ownable intelligence is the raw material. The investor sees a low-margin commodity and walks away. The engineer sees a free input and asks: what can I finally build now that this costs nothing? Same fact, opposite reactions — and only one of them ships anything.

So let me get concrete about what building in an agent-shaped world actually looks like, because "software becomes agents" is very easy to say and very easy to get wrong.

Start with this: the interface inverts. The oldest assumption in software is that a human drives and the software responds. You click, it reacts. Every screen, every form, every menu is scaffolding for a person operating a machine. The agent flips that. The software drives, and the human sets the intent and supervises. That's not a fresh coat of paint on the user interface — it's a different center of gravity for the entire system. The screen stops being the product. The product becomes the loop: perceive, decide, act, check. And the screen is just one window you can peek into that loop through. In practice, all that effort you used to pour into the front end — the state management, the wizard flows, the careful choreography of what the user's allowed to click next — most of that evaporates or moves. What replaces it is designing the agent's capability surface: the set of actions it can take, described well enough that a model picks between them correctly, with guardrails so a wrong choice isn't a catastrophe. And that's harder than the front end it replaces, because your "user" now has no eyes, no common sense, and no hesitation.

Which leads to the next shift: features stop being screens and start being tools. In the old model, a feature is something you build and then teach a human to find — a button, a settings page, a documented workflow. In the agent model, a feature is a tool the agent can reach for. And the whole discipline of exposing tools to a model is exactly the terrain I keep writing about. Whether you reach for MCP or a plain command-line skill isn't a plumbing decision underneath the value — it is the product surface. Get the tool descriptions wrong, and the smartest model in the world will call the wrong capability at the wrong moment. Get the permission boundaries wrong, and you've handed an access token to something with no judgment about when to use it. This is where the five-layer map fails an engineer most concretely: there's no line item for the contract between the agent and the world — and that contract is where most of the real engineering, and most of the real defensibility, now lives.

So where would I actually spend the effort? A few places, in order. First, the capability layer — the tools, their descriptions, their boundaries, their failure modes. That's the new API design — the application programming interface — and it's the highest-leverage code you'll write. Second, the data loop. And I mean the loop, not data as some static asset: the agent acts, you capture the outcome, and that outcome makes the next action better. An agent with a tight loop on your specific problem beats a bigger model with none. That's a moat you build, not one you buy. Third, reliability and evals — the unglamorous scaffolding that tells you whether the agent is getting better or quietly getting worse. In an agent product, your test suite is a behavioral one, and building it is most of the job. And fourth, model-agnosticism. Precisely because the intelligence layer is commoditizing, treat the model as a swappable part. The day I ran Gemma on a Mac, I wasn't proving a Mac can host a model — I was proving the model is a component I can source three different ways. Build so that swapping it is a config change, not a rewrite, and the commoditization the article mourns turns into leverage you own. And notice what's not on that list: owning the silicon, training a frontier model, betting the company on a fab. Those are capital plays. They belong to people with capital. The engineer's plays are all further up — and they compound with judgment and data, which is the one kind of moat that gets deeper the cheaper the hardware underneath it gets.

Now the sharpest way to feel this shift: look at what you actually sell. SaaS sells software — a tool your customer's team logs into and operates. Agent SaaS sells work. You take a job the customer's team currently does, you package the whole thing, and you sell the outcome as a service. The team stops operating a tool and starts handing off a responsibility. Sounds like a small rewording. It changes everything. The buyer stops evaluating features and seat counts and starts asking a much simpler question — "will this reliably get the job done?" — the same question they'd ask when hiring a person. And the builder stops shipping capabilities and hoping the customer assembles them into value. You're now on the hook for the value itself. That's a heavier promise — and it's exactly why the margin follows it back up the stack. Nobody pays commodity prices for something that shows up, does the work, and is accountable for the result. And this is why the commoditization the article mourns is actually a tailwind here. When the intelligence underneath is cheap and abundant, the cost of doing the job collapses — but the price you can charge is anchored to the human labor you're replacing, not to your compute bill. The gap between those two numbers is the whole business.

And this pattern is already shipping. The tell is that these products don't describe themselves as software at all — they describe themselves as a role. There's a company called Slang AI that sells, basically, an AI super-host for restaurants. It answers the phone, manages reservations, routes the VIPs to a human, pings the staff when something needs attention. Nobody at the restaurant logs into a dashboard to "use the reservation feature" — the phone just gets answered, all of it, every time. The product is the host. There's another one, Same Day, doing the equivalent for home services: AI dispatchers and receptionists that answer calls, book jobs, reschedule — without a human touching them. Again, the sale isn't a tool. It's the front desk. And what makes both of them work is the bar they clear: handle one genuinely annoying job better than a junior hire, faster than an agency, and cheaper than new headcount. That's the whole value proposition — and notice it's measured against people, not against other software. You don't benchmark a SaaS tool against a receptionist. You benchmark an agent against one, because it's doing the receptionist's job. Both of those are linked in the show notes.

[OUTRO]
So here's the prognosis, flipped. Yes — software ate the world, and now hardware is eating software. But something always crawls out of the thing that got eaten. This time, it's the agent. And it's standing on all that cheap silicon, using it — not being replaced by it. The article looked down the stack and saw where the capital sinks. Look up the stack, and you see where the work goes. And the work is where builders have always, eventually, captured the value. Point software is dying. Long live the agent. If you want the full written version, with all the links and the numbers, it's on the blog — check the show notes. I'm Sam, thanks for listening, and I'll catch you in the next one.
